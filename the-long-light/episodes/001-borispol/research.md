## Working title
**The Last Pilot at Borispol**

### Title alternates
1. The Last Hours of the Soviet Sky
2. December 25, 1991: A Quiet Evening at Borispol

## Positioning memo (the angle gap)
Existing coverage of the Soviet collapse is overwhelmingly geopolitical or biographical — Gorbachev's resignation speech, Yeltsin's rise, the Belavezha Accords. Almost nobody tells the story from inside an ordinary day inside an ordinary airport in an ordinary city, on the evening the country ended. *That* is The Long Light's wedge: the dissolution as it was experienced — slowly, awkwardly, by people who had been told to wait.

## Sourced facts (dissolution-day timeline)

> Tag = visual signal:
>   `[VERIFIED]` cross-checked against multiple historical sources
>   `[CONTEXT]` widely accepted historical fact
>   `[COMPOSITE]` representative narrative element, framed as such on screen

1. **`[VERIFIED]` Mikhail Gorbachev resigned as President of the USSR on the evening of 25 December 1991, in a televised address from the Kremlin.**
   - Gorbachev resignation speech, public record (text widely reproduced)
   - NYT, Washington Post, BBC archives, 25–26 Dec 1991

2. **`[VERIFIED]` The Soviet flag was lowered from the Kremlin at approximately 19:32 Moscow time on 25 December 1991, replaced with the flag of the Russian Federation.**
   - Multiple wire-service accounts
   - Photographic record

3. **`[VERIFIED]` On 8 December 1991, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus signed the Belavezha Accords at the Viskuli hunting lodge in the Belarusian forest, declaring that the Soviet Union "as a subject of international law and as a geopolitical reality, ceases to exist."**
   - Text of the accords, public record
   - Yeltsin and Kravchuk memoirs

4. **`[VERIFIED]` On 21 December 1991, eleven of the twelve remaining Soviet republics signed the Alma-Ata Protocol, formally creating the Commonwealth of Independent States and confirming the dissolution of the USSR.**
   - Text of the protocol, public record

5. **`[VERIFIED]` On 26 December 1991, the Soviet of the Republics — the upper chamber of the Soviet legislature — passed declaration №142-Н, which formally acknowledged the USSR's extinction.**
   - Russian state archives, public

6. **`[VERIFIED]` Ukraine had declared independence on 24 August 1991. A binding referendum on 1 December 1991 confirmed it with 92.3% of the vote.**
   - Verkhovna Rada records
   - Central Election Commission, Ukraine

7. **`[CONTEXT]` Borispol International Airport (now spelled Boryspil) was — and remains — the main international civilian airport for Kyiv. In late 1991 it operated under Aeroflot's central scheduling, with most domestic flights using Tupolev Tu-134 medium-range twin-jets.**
   - Airport history — public record
   - Aeroflot fleet records, late Soviet period

8. **`[CONTEXT]` Aeroflot, the Soviet state airline, was the world's largest by some measures in the 1980s, with operations spanning all 15 Soviet republics. Its dissolution into national carriers happened gradually after December 1991, with Ukrainian operations passing to what became Air Ukraine in 1992.**
   - ICAO archives
   - Air Ukraine corporate history

9. **`[CONTEXT]` On 25 December 1991, the formal handover of the Soviet "nuclear briefcase" — the Cheget — from Gorbachev to Yeltsin took place at the Kremlin, after the resignation speech.**
   - Multiple memoirs (Yeltsin, Gorbachev, military aides)
   - Documented in *Collapse: The Fall of the Soviet Union*, Vladislav Zubok (2021)

10. **`[CONTEXT]` Throughout late 1991, civilian and military flight operations continued across the dissolving Union. Crews reported uncertainty about chain of command, fuel allocation, and which ministry's orders applied — but flights continued, and no incidents are recorded for the evening of 25 December specifically.**
    - Aviation historiography, late-Soviet period
    - Russian and Ukrainian aviation memoir literature

## Composite narrative framing (on screen + in VO)
- The runway scene is presented as *representative* of countless similar moments across the Union that evening. The on-screen text and VO will use language like *"on a runway like this one"*, *"the captain we are imagining was real, in this sense — every Aeroflot Tu-134 captain that night faced the same uncertainty"*.
- We will NOT name an individual pilot or claim a specific aircraft tail number for the runway scene unless a primary source surfaces in the Playwright pass.
- We WILL name and date every dissolution-day fact (Gorbachev's speech, the flag, Belavezha, Alma-Ata, the referendum).

## What this episode will not claim
- That a specific named pilot was on a specific tarmac at a specific minute.
- That Aeroflot operations were paused, suspended, or otherwise disrupted on 25 December 1991. They were not — flights continued.
- That the dissolution was experienced uniformly. It was not.
