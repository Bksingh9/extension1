## Title (working)
**The Day Iran Closed the Strait**

### Alternates
1. 33 Kilometres of Water: How Iran Locked Up 20% of the World's Oil
2. The Hormuz Files — Oil, LNG, and Internet Through One Channel
3. Hormuz: The Chokepoint Iran Finally Closed

[SCENE 1 — Hook — 0:00–0:10]
On the 28th of February, 2026, Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz. Thirty-four kilometres of water. One fifth of the world's oil. And, it turns out, more of the internet than almost anybody realised.

[SCENE 2 — Chokepoint — 0:10–0:30]
The Strait of Hormuz is the channel of water between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula. At its narrowest point, the International Energy Agency measures it at thirty-four kilometres across — about the distance from one side of London to the other. Through that gap pass the oil tankers carrying crude from Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, and Iran itself, out to refineries on three other continents.

[SCENE 3 — Oil — 0:30–0:55]
In 2024, the United States Energy Information Administration measured the average daily flow at twenty million barrels of oil. That is one fifth of all the petroleum products consumed by the planet, and more than a quarter of all seaborne oil trade. Saudi Arabia alone sent five and a half million barrels a day through the Strait — thirty-eight per cent of the total. Add liquefied natural gas, and another fifth of the global LNG trade — almost all of it Qatari — moved through the same gap. Through one channel, every day.

[SCENE 4 — Cables — 0:55–1:30]
Underneath those tankers, on the floor of the Gulf, lies the second chokepoint. The Asia–Africa–Europe-1 cable, with landings in the Emirates, Oman, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia. The FALCON network, linking India and Sri Lanka into the Gulf and onwards to Sudan and Egypt. The Gulf Bridge International cable, which connects every Gulf country including Iran. Three of the seventeen subsea cables routed through the region. According to TeleGeography, every one of them was deliberately laid in Omani waters — to keep them away from Iran. Until February of 2026, that worked.

[SCENE 5 — Closure — 1:30–2:30]
The closure was a consequence, not a cause. On the morning of the twenty-eighth of February, 2026, an Israeli airstrike — built on intelligence supplied by the United States — killed Iran's Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, in his office in Tehran. The Iranian government confirmed his death the following day. The strike also killed members of his immediate family, including his daughter, Boshra, his son-in-law Mesbah Bagheri Kani, and his daughter-in-law Zahra Haddad-Adel. On the same day, Iran began turning shipping back at the Strait. By the first of March, the chokepoint, in practical terms, was closed.

[SCENE 6 — Oil consequence — 2:30–3:30]
What does it mean to close one fifth of the world's oil? Brent crude has historically moved by eight to ten per cent for every one per cent of seaborne supply that comes off the market. A full Hormuz closure, even a temporary one, removes roughly twenty per cent. We are not predicting prices. We are reporting the structural relationships. Bypass pipelines exist — the Saudi East-West line, and the Emirati Habshan-to-Fujairah line — but together they can move only seven million barrels a day, against the twenty that normally pass the Strait. The math, the EIA wrote in 2024, "would have huge consequences for world oil markets." It is no longer hypothetical.

[SCENE 7 — Internet consequence — 3:30–4:20]
The cable consequence is harder to see and possibly more permanent. A subsea cable cut in shallow water can be repaired in days. A cut in deep, mined, or contested water is slower — sometimes weeks, sometimes months. In April 2026, Iranian state-affiliated media — through outlets aligned with the IRGC — publicly identified subsea cables as a vulnerability of the regional digital economy. As of late April, no cable cut has been confirmed as the work of Iran during this crisis. The threat, however, is now in the public record. Before this, there was no faceless, six-minute, citation-first explainer that put oil flow and cable maps on the same chokepoint diagram. There is now. Filed under amber, until something is cut.

[SCENE 8 — Why nobody stops them — 4:20–5:00]
There are exactly five navies in the world capable of contesting the Strait at scale: the United States Fifth Fleet, the Royal Navy, the French Marine Nationale, the Indian Navy, and Iran's own. Iran's coast is six hundred miles of mountain and missile site. Force-projecting an oil tanker through a strait under aerial surveillance from a hostile shore is, every naval planner agrees, a very different problem from defending a friendly port. That is why "Iran could close it" stayed hypothetical for fifty years. The deterrent was not military superiority. It was the assumption that Iran would not pay the price of closing it.

[SCENE 9 — Aftermath — 5:00–5:35]
On the eighth of March, 2026, the Iranian Assembly of Experts convened and elected Ayatollah Khamenei's son, Mojtaba Khamenei, as Supreme Leader. The strait, as of recording, remains closed. The cables, as of recording, remain intact. The oil bypass pipelines, at full output, cover roughly a third of normal Hormuz volume. The map has not changed. The price of crossing it has.

[SCENE 10 — Filed — 5:35–6:00]
What still does not exist on the public record: an Iranian government statement of the closure rules of engagement, a confirmed cable cut, a successor's foreign-policy doctrine. We will report each of those when they exist. The next dossier covers the program the C.I.A. ran on prisoners of war in Korea. The documents have just been declassified. Subscribe to read them with us. Filed.
