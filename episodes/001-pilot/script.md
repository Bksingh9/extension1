## Title (working)
**Operation Gladio: NATO's Secret War Inside Its Own Borders**

### Alternates
1. The Stay-Behinds: Inside NATO's 45-Year Secret Army
2. Gladio: The Day Italy Admitted Its Army Was Real

---

[SCENE 1 — Hook — 0:00–0:08]
On the 24th of October, 1990, the Prime Minister of Italy stood up in parliament and admitted his government had been running a secret army for forty-five years. Its name was Gladio.

[SCENE 2 — Doctrine — 0:08–0:25]
The story does not start in Italy. It starts in 1947, with NATO and a doctrine called "stay-behind". The idea was simple: if the Soviets ever rolled west, leave armed cells inside every NATO country to fight the occupation from underground. Cells with weapons. Cells with codenames. Cells with orders.

[SCENE 3 — Network — 0:25–0:45]
In Italy alone, when the network was finally disclosed, prosecutors counted six hundred and twenty-two civilian members, and one hundred and twenty-seven dismantled arms caches. Belgium had one. So did the Netherlands. So did Norway, Denmark, Greece, Turkey, France, Switzerland, Austria, Portugal, Spain, West Germany — and, later disclosures showed, Sweden and Finland.

[SCENE 4 — Pivot — 0:45–1:00]
Officially, they were there to fight Soviets who never came. What they actually did, between 1947 and 1990, is the rest of this dossier. We are going to walk through it the way the documents do — what is proven, what is alleged, and what is still classified.

[SCENE 5 — Memoir — 1:00–1:45]
It began with a CIA officer named William Colby. In his 1978 memoir *Honorable Men*, Colby wrote about helping build the stay-behind networks — first in Sweden, where he was posted from nineteen fifty-one, then in Italy, from nineteen fifty-three. The cells were buried with weapons. They were trained for a war that never came. They were also, the documents show, paid for and directed in part by the United States.

[SCENE 6 — Andreotti — 1:45–2:45]
Forty-five years went by before any government talked about it. On the twenty-fourth of October, 1990, Italian Prime Minister Giulio Andreotti described what he called, in his own words, "a structure of information, response and safeguard." The structure had six hundred and twenty-two civilian members. One hundred and twenty-seven arms caches had been dismantled. He insisted Gladio had never been involved in any of the bombings of the Italian "years of lead". One month later, on the twenty-second of November, 1990, the European Parliament passed a resolution condemning the existence of stay-behind networks across the continent — networks that, the resolution said, "escaped all democratic controls."

[SCENE 7 — Bologna — 2:45–3:45]
The bombing the Italian Prime Minister insisted Gladio was not part of — was the worst attack in modern Italian history. On the second of August, 1980, at twenty-five past ten in the morning, a time-bomb left in a suitcase exploded in the second-class waiting room of Bologna Centrale station. Eighty-five people were killed. Two hundred more were wounded. Italian courts spent forty years convicting five neo-fascist militants for placing the bomb. Then, in January 2021, the Bologna Court of Appeal issued its written ruling. It called the bombing, quote, "a State massacre." And it named, as one of its masterminders, an Italian intelligence official named Federico Umberto D'Amato — who, the court found, "had links with NATO, the Stay-Behind, the Gladio organisation."

[SCENE 8 — Belgium — 3:45–4:45]
And not only Italy. Belgium had its own network — codename SDRA-VIII, sitting under Belgian military intelligence. The Belgian government disclosed it on the seventh of November, 1990. Between 1982 and 1985, a series of armed attacks across the Belgian province of Brabant killed twenty-eight people and wounded twenty-two. Their perpetrators were never identified. Investigators raised the possibility of a stay-behind link. The Belgian parliamentary inquiry concluded it could find no substantive evidence connecting SDRA-VIII to the killings. The Brabant case remains officially open. Forty years later.

[SCENE 9 — Disclosed — 4:45–5:30]
Country by country, the disclosures came. 1990 — Italy. Belgium. Switzerland. 1991 — France, Denmark, Greece. 1996 — Norway, after the Lund Commission. In the year 2000, the Italian Senate published its final report and concluded, in its majority view, that some massacres of the late 1960s through the 1980s had been carried out "with the collaboration of the structures of information of the United States." Sweden waited until 2014 to confirm that its own stay-behind network, "Sveaborg," had been active until at least 1990.

[SCENE 10 — Filed — 5:30–6:00]
What we still do not know — what the British network actually did, what Turkey's "Counter-Guerrilla" was for, what is still classified in the archives of fourteen countries — is in folders we have not seen. The next dossier covers the false-flag the Pentagon wrote down in 1962. They called it Operation Northwoods. President Kennedy told them no. The paperwork survives. Subscribe to read it with us.
